Reflection

Evidence on this page: the Ticket 101, Ticket 102 and Ticket 103 tabs (Expected vs Actual) and the Cash tab. "seq" numbers point into output/audit_trail.json (the Problem 9 run is seq 484–648).

1. How I would evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why

I graded each ticket on five things, in this order. I'm running a shop, not a demo, so getting the money and the customer right matters more than speed:

  1. Right decision: is the call correct given what's actually in the database?
  2. Money safety: did cash move only after my approval, by exactly the right amount?
  3. Customer outcome: is the draft honest, specific and free of internal details?
  4. Efficiency: MCP calls and runs versus my plan.
  5. Robustness: did anything fail, and was the failure visible?

Why 102 is the weakest, even though it was the easiest ticket. The resolution is backed by the database (payment #2 posted, request #2 paid, lease moved to 2026-10-02), but a ticket this simple shouldn't need me to explain an exception. It also exposed an earlier logic gap. In Problem 8, a re-run after paying the rent saw next_due jump a month and flagged it as a "discrepancy". 2. How Actual compared to my Expected plan, ticket by ticket

Ticket 101. My plan was right about the shape: Inventory first, then Accounting, then Customer Service, and no Facilities. Two things differed. The Boss sent Inventory and Accounting in the same step (seq 490–491) instead of one after the other. In hindsight that was reasonable, because the linked invoice made the money question independent of the stock answer. And I planned ~7 calls ending at waiting_approval. The first run did end at waiting_approval, but with 13 calls: each specialist re-read the ticket, and Accounting called get_vendor_status and list_payment_requests on top of the get_cash_position + request_payment I expected. My plan also stopped at "waiting on a human". It never said how the ticket gets closed, which took a second run (6 calls) and a rewritten draft #2. I skipped the optional list_inventory, and so did the agents.

Ticket 102. This was the closest match. I said Facilities first to verify before anyone touches money, and that's exactly what happened. Accounting filed the rent next, sequentially as planned, and neither Inventory nor Customer Service ran. The differences were small. I expected Facilities to call list_payment_requests, but Accounting did it instead. I expected Facilities might hand off to Accounting directly, but the Boss did the relay. I expected ~5 calls and got 9 + 3. My plan's "Paying it would move the lease's next due date to 2026-10-02" turned out to be literally true, and it was also the thing that confused the agents until we gave them last_paid_due_date.

Ticket 103. The delegation order matched my plan exactly (Inventory → Accounting → Customer Service, no Facilities). The situation didn't. I wrote the plan as if each ticket started from a fresh database, so I expected Bulldog Print Co to still be blocked by invoice 501 and the ticket to end blocked. In the real run, 103 went last: 501 had already been paid for ticket 101 and rent for 102, so the vendor could ship and the only constraint was the $160 in checking. My planned draft offered "8 now, or all 20 at $49.30 once a restock is possible". The actual draft offered only the 8 at list. That's firmer and more honest, because with no revenue in this app a restock never becomes possible. Calls: 17 vs ~7, but in one run.

The pattern across all three: my plans got who and in what order right every time (first specialist: 3/3; no unneeded agent: 3/3). They were wrong about how much work (2–3× the MCP calls) and about the process around a human approval (101 and 102 each needed a second run to close). I planned tickets in isolation, but they share one checking account, so the order 101 → 102 → 103 changed what 103 could do.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why

Ticket 102, the rent notice. It is a straight line with no judgment calls between specialties: read the ticket → read the lease and cash (get_rent_status) → confirm priority (get_cash_position) → request_payment(kind="rent", ref_id=1) → update_ticket. One agent with those five tools could do it in about 5 calls and one model loop. In our run it took three agents, 12 calls and two runs:

  • The Facilities → Accounting hop was a relay, not a collaboration. Facilities' report ("landlord, $2,400 and 2026-09-02 all match") was turned into a task for Accounting, which then re-read the same ticket and called get_rent_status(1) again (seq 575). Every handoff starts a new agent with an empty memory, so facts get fetched twice.
  • More model calls means more chances to fail. Both gateway incidents in Problem 9 hit the Boss's turn after update_ticket (the 101 hang after seq 526, the 102 crash after seq 594). A single agent has fewer of those turns.
  • The safety argument for separate agents is weak here. The real protection for rent isn't "Facilities can't file payments". It's that approve_and_pay isn't available to any agent and requires my name. One agent with request_payment would have been exactly as safe.

Where the team did earn its cost was ticket 103. Stock (Inventory), pricing and cash priority (Accounting) and customer wording (Customer Service) each needed different rules, and the dedicated prompts held them. Accounting declined the $264 restock, and Customer Service's checklist kept the vendor and the $160 balance out of the club's draft. Ticket 101 sits in between: the invoice-501 insight came from Inventory's prompt telling it to read invoice descriptions, which one agent could also follow.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools we built

Each assumes the request lands on the board as a ticket. Today that means a row in tickets, since there's no create-ticket route.

  1. A customer wants Crest mugs, which are out of stock. Say someone orders 12 × CC-MUG-CREST. Inventory: check_stock("CC-MUG-CREST","OS",12) → 0 on hand, short 12. get_vendor_status() matches "mugs and small goods" to Elm City Gifts (3-day lead, no open invoices, can ship). Accounting: get_cash_position() → $160.00, empty queue, so a restock of 12 × $3.50 = $42.00 fits and leaves $118.00. It files request_payment(kind="purchase_order", vendor_id=2, sku="CC-MUG-CREST", size="OS", qty=12), I approve it, and approve_and_pay returns an expected arrival 3 days out. Customer Service drafts "about 3 days". Unlike ticket 103, this restock is affordable, so the same agents would say yes.
  2. A fake rent notice. An email from "Elm City Property Mgmt LLC" asks for the shop's $2,400 rent "to our new account". Facilities' prompt already says to compare the requester with leases.landlord (Elm City Properties) and treat a mismatch as possible phishing. get_rent_status(1) also shows rent_payments_on_file (payment #2) and last_paid_due_date 2026-09-02, so September is already paid and the next due date is 2026-10-02. The team would set the ticket to blocked with a fraud flag and file nothing. Ticket notes are treated as data, so "pay today" in the email can't instruct anyone.
  3. A discount request above our cap, or a bulk order we can fill. Say the Yale AI Club comes back for 30 × CC-HAT-BLUE caps at 25% off. check_stock → 40 on hand, so we can fill it. get_pricing("CC-HAT-BLUE",30,25) → within_policy: false (max 15% for 10+). At 15% it's $18.70/cap, a $12.70 margin on a $6 cost. Accounting recommends 15% and the Boss sets waiting_approval so I decide whether to go beyond policy. No cash moves, the draft quotes $561 for 30 caps at 15% off, and the agents can't grant 25% on their own.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve, and what we'd need

  1. October rent is coming, and the shop can't pay it. The Cash tab ends at $160.00, and leases.next_due is now 2026-10-02 for $2,400.00: a $2,240 hole. Our system can only spend money. cash_accounts has no inflows, the Yale AI Club's $464 for 8 hoodies has nowhere to be recorded, and nothing looks ahead. Accounting would only notice when the next rent ticket arrives, and then approve_and_pay would refuse for insufficient cash.
    What we'd need: a receivables/sales table and tools record_customer_payment(ticket_id, amount) and record_sale(sku,size,qty,price) that credit checking (human-confirmed, the mirror image of approvals). Also a forecast_cash(days=30) tool that lists every known obligation (rent, open invoices, approved POs) against expected inflows. And a Finance/Treasury agent that runs weekly, not only when a ticket arrives. It would flag the October shortfall now and propose options (collect the club's $464, delay restocks, ask the landlord for terms) for a human to choose.
  2. Closing the loop on ticket 101: the reprint actually arrives. We paid invoice 501 so Bulldog Print Co would ship the size S tee (5-day lead → about Sept 5), but no tool can record that a shipment arrived. inventory for CC-TEE-WHITE S will stay at 0 forever, Tauhid's tee is never reserved, and if 8 hoodies sell to the club, check_stock will still say 8. Ticket 101 is "resolved" for the office, not for the customer.
    What we'd need: orders / order_lines and purchase_orders tables, plus write tools receive_shipment(po_or_invoice_id, qty), reserve_stock(ticket_id, sku, size, qty) and fulfill_order(order_id), all of which update inventory.qty with an audit row. A Fulfillment/Receiving agent would watch expected arrivals (from vendors.lead_days), ask a person to confirm the box arrived, then reserve stock for waiting tickets and reopen them for a "your tee is here" draft.
  3. Talking to people: sending drafts and reading replies. Drafts #2 and #3 are sitting on the board. Nothing sends them, and nothing would notice if the club replied "yes, we'll take the 8". The same goes for vendors: to know whether Bulldog Print Co could rush 12 more hoodies cheaper, or to confirm the tee's ship date, someone has to email them. Today every ticket must be typed into the database by hand. This is deliberate for the homework (no emails, no Outlook MCP), but a real shop lives in its inbox.
    What we'd need: an inbox/outbox integration (e.g. the Outlook MCP) behind two new tools: send_draft(draft_id, approved_by), human-approved exactly like payments, and ingest_message(...). An Intake agent would turn incoming emails into tickets, link replies to the existing ticket (draft #3 → the club's answer → reopen 103) and treat message text as data, never as instructions. A Vendor Relations agent would ask suppliers for quotes and ship dates, but could never promise payment.
